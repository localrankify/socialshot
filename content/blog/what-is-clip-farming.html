"Clip farming" went from a streamer insult to a job title in about two years. If you have seen people talk about getting paid to clip streams, or a streamer accused of clip farming, this guide explains what the term means, how the money works, and how to clip properly.

Clip farming has three meanings

  1. Engineering moments. A streamer does something outrageous on purpose so it gets clipped. This is the original, unflattering use: "he's just clip farming".
  2. Repurposing your own content. A creator cuts one long stream or podcast into dozens of short clips to post everywhere.
  3. Paid reposting. Clippers cut other people's content into short videos and get paid for the views those videos get.

In practice, people increasingly use "clip farming" for the spammy end: unlicensed, unedited, mass-reposted clips. "Clipping" or "campaign clipping" is used for the legitimate version, where the content is licensed and each post is edited.

How paid clipping works

The legitimate version runs through clipping campaigns (see our full guide to how clipping campaigns work). A streamer, podcast or brand publishes a campaign that says:

  • which streams, episodes or footage you may clip,
  • how to credit them and which tags or links to include,
  • which platforms count, and how views are verified,
  • how much they pay per thousand verified views.

Clippers find campaigns on marketplaces, post clips from their own accounts, and get paid on the views. Rates differ a lot between campaigns: one round-up reported marketplace campaigns paying from around $0.20 to $6 per 1,000 verified views. Treat any figure as a range, not a promise, since most published numbers come from companies that run clipping platforms.

Big streamers now spend serious money on clippers. One widely reported case had a streamer paying hundreds of clippers to cut his streams into shorts over a few weeks. That demand is why clipping looks like a job, and also why platforms are watching for spam.

What separates clipping from spam

  • Permission. Only clip content you are allowed to clip. Campaigns give you that in writing.
  • Editing. A tight cut, a hook, captions and a vertical frame. A raw rip with black bars is what gets clips taken down.
  • Selection. Ten good clips beat a hundred random ones. Platforms reward watch time, not volume.
  • Honesty. No fake titles, no misleading thumbnails, no stolen moments presented as your own.

How to start clipping

  1. Pick a niche you actually watch: one game, one kind of podcast, one sport. You will spot good moments faster.
  2. Join a campaign or ask a smaller creator directly. Many are happy to have clips made of their streams.
  3. Set up your tools. A live stream clipper records the streams you are allowed to clip and saves the moments as they happen, so you are not watching every hour live.
  4. Edit for the feed. Vertical, captioned, starting on the moment. See auto captions.
  5. Post consistently and keep the views verifiable, the way each campaign asks.

Is clip farming worth it?

For streamers, clipping is now one of the main ways new viewers find you. For clippers, it can pay, but income depends on campaigns, consistency and quality, and the rules keep changing. Do it with permission, edit every post, and it is a real skill. Do it as mass reposting, and it is the clip farming people complain about.
